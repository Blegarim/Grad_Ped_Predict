\begin{abstract}
Pedestrian crossing prediction is usually benchmarked with an event-anchored
protocol that samples a few windows per pedestrian one to two seconds before an
annotated crossing. However, a vehicle-mounted detector watches continuously
and must decide at every moment whether a crossing is about to begin. On the
Pedestrian Intention Estimation (PIE) dataset, the anchored protocol yields
about $2.5$ negative windows per positive, whereas streaming sampling yields
$33.9$. In the streaming test set, $39\%$ of windows show a pedestrian who
crosses only after the one-second horizon, a kind of negative absent from the anchored protocol. A model trained under the anchored protocol drops from
$0.89$ to $0.52$ area under the ROC curve (AUC) on streaming data, and threshold
re-tuning recovers almost none of this loss. The same model separates crossing from non-crossing pedestrians ($0.80$ AUC) but ranks imminent crossings below
distant ones. We then predict the onset time of a crossing, so that windows whose footage ends early are treated as censored observations instead of negatives. The model still reports the probability of a crossing within the original horizon. Across three training seeds per method, the detection rates of the two
formulations at fixed false-alarm rates lie within the seed-to-seed spread.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A pedestrian-crossing detector on a moving vehicle works as a streaming system. It judges at every moment, for every pedestrian in view, whether someone is about to step into the road, even though crossings are rare events. The protocol used to evaluate such systems asks a narrower question.
This protocol came with the Pedestrian Intention Estimation (PIE) and Joint Attention in Autonomous Driving (JAAD) datasets~\cite{rasouli2019pie,rasouli2017jaad}, and a later evaluation suite~\cite{kotseruba2021benchmark} made it standard. It takes a few labelled windows from each pedestrian track, with their last frames one to two seconds before the crossing event, where observation stops. The resulting class ratio
is low, about $2.5$ negatives per positive ($2.5{:}1$), and reported scores are high.

The two settings differ in more than difficulty. Because the anchored protocol
places its windows relative to a known event, it skips the earlier windows of a crossing pedestrian's track, in which the person is still walking
along the pavement (\cref{fig:protocols}). Under a short horizon these windows are negatives that look almost identical to the positives following them. A deployed detector has to stay silent on them. The protocol leaves these windows out of both training and testing, so models never see them in either. This paper measures that omission and its cost.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{fig1_protocols.pdf}
  \caption{The two sampling protocols on one pedestrian track, with (a)~event-anchored windows ending a known interval before a known crossing onset and (b)~streaming windows covering the whole track, including negatives of the same pedestrian before the onset (drawn further apart than in practice, where windows are $20$ frames long and start every $3$ frames).}
  \label{fig:protocols}
\end{figure*}

This paper makes five contributions.
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{Protocol analysis.} The anchored class ratio measures how many
  pedestrians eventually cross, and the streaming ratio measures how often a
  crossing is about to begin (\cref{sec:protocols}).
  \item \textbf{Composition of the streaming negatives.} The PIE training and
  test splits contain different shares of negatives from pedestrians who cross
  later, and most of these lie far from the horizon (\cref{sec:omits}).
  \item \textbf{Cross-protocol diagnosis.} Threshold re-tuning recovers
  almost none of the anchored-to-streaming gap, and the anchored-trained model also identifies who crosses but orders the timing of their crossings in
  reverse (\cref{sec:gap}).
  \item \textbf{Onset timing under right-censoring.} A discrete-time hazard
  gives, for each future interval, the probability that a crossing starts there
  given that none has started earlier. The loss ignores unobserved intervals. Because the model still reports the probability of a crossing within the original horizon, it stays comparable with binary baselines (\cref{sec:method}).
  \item \textbf{Evaluation at a $33.9{:}1$ base rate.} The \FmeanGap{} gap
  between the two objectives' mean F1 barely exceeds the sum of their standard deviations, and smoothing the scores along each track moves AUC by about as much as the objectives differ. We also compare detection rate at matched false-alarm budgets, as in quickest change detection~\cite{page1954cusum}. By a criterion fixed in advance, the objectives tie on this measure too, and the binary baseline warns earlier
  (\cref{sec:experiments}).
\end{enumerate}
Streaming evaluation itself is, however, well established in online action detection~\cite{degeest2016oad,shou2018odas,gao2019startnet}. Our claim is only that the crossing-intention literature has not adopted it, that this has a measurable cost, and that a timing formulation addresses the labelling
problem directly.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Crossing Intention Estimation on PIE and JAAD}
PIE~\cite{rasouli2019pie} and JAAD~\cite{rasouli2017jaad} are the standard datasets, and most later work uses the evaluation protocol of Kotseruba \emph{et al.}~\cite{kotseruba2021benchmark} largely unchanged, with per-track binary
labels, observation clipped at the crossing point and a time-to-event interval
of one to two seconds. Azarmi \emph{et al.}~\cite{azarmi2024feature} split PIE into context sets and report a strong dependence on ego-vehicle speed. In addition, Rasouli and Kotseruba~\cite{rasouli2024diving} revise the task definitions and add new types of metric. Both keep the window population of the anchored protocol, the population this paper questions. Yao \emph{et
al.}~\cite{yao2021coupling} come closest to our work. They report results for an ``event-to-crossing'' setting, with sequences from the one to two seconds before the crossing, and for an ``original data'' setting that uses the whole track, and note that the first contains more changes in action. We instead label a window by whether an onset falls within a fixed horizon and study the early windows that the event-to-crossing setting omits.

\subsection{Online Action Detection}
Online action detection labels each frame of a stream using present and past
evidence only~\cite{degeest2016oad}. Its onset-focused variant, action start
detection~\cite{shou2018odas,gao2019startnet}, has to signal a beginning as
early as possible in untrimmed video dominated by background frames, mostly
with transformer-based
methods~\cite{wang2021oadtr,xu2021lstr,zhao2022testra,wang2023mat} and with
evaluation tools such as calibrated and point-level average precision. Shou
\emph{et al.}~\cite{shou2018odas} note that a start window shares scene and
objects with the preceding frames, which places its features closer to the background than to the action that follows. A window taken just before someone
steps off the kerb also resembles that person waiting more than it
resembles a crossing. Pedestrian crossing is often cited as a motivating
example in this literature, although its evaluations are run on television, sports and ego-vehicle manoeuvre data.

\subsection{Time-to-Event Modelling}
Survival analysis models the time until an event when some observations end
before the event occurs~\cite{kaplan1958nonparametric,cox1972regression}. The per-interval hazard with a masked binary likelihood used in \cref{sec:method} is standard there and has been carried over to neural estimators~\cite{lee2018deephit}. Kalatian and
Farooq~\cite{kalatian2019deepwait} apply survival modelling to pedestrian
waiting times at unsignalised crossings, using a neural Cox model trained on
virtual-reality participant data, which is a different setting and quantity
from onset detection in in-vehicle video. A targeted but non-exhaustive search
found no prior work applying censored time-to-event modelling to crossing onset
under the PIE protocol. The contribution here is the observation that
streaming crossing windows already form a right-censored dataset, and that
binary labelling throws away the censoring information.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:protocols}

\subsection{Event-Anchored Sampling}
Consider a track with annotated frames $1,\dots,n$, and let $\tau$ denote the
frame at which a crossing pedestrian begins to cross. Under the anchored protocol, observation stops at $\tau$ (or at the last visible frame for non-crossing pedestrians). A few windows of length $T$ are then extracted from each track, with their final frame in a fixed interval before $\tau$, typically $30$ to $60$ frames. The label is assigned per track, i.e., it records whether the pedestrian crosses at all. The share of positives thus reflects how many pedestrians eventually cross, giving roughly $2.5{:}1$ on PIE.

\subsection{Streaming Sampling}
In the streaming protocol, a window is placed at every stride-$s$ position along
the whole track. The label of a window ending at frame $t$ is
\begin{equation}
  y_t = \mathbf{1}\left[\, \exists\ \text{onset in } (t,\, t+H] \,\right],
  \label{eq:binlabel}
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the anticipation horizon. Windows in which a crossing is already
underway are discarded, because the onset they would have to anticipate has
already happened. The share of positives then measures how often a crossing
is imminent at a given observed moment, which on PIE is the case for $2.9\%$ of
the windows (a ratio of $33.9{:}1$).

Because the anchored protocol draws the same small number of windows from each
track, its $2.5{:}1$ describes the balance across tracks, whereas the streaming
$33.9{:}1$ is a rate over time. The two ratios thus estimate different quantities. Neither setting is a harder or easier version of the other. One might object
that streaming sampling is anchored sampling with a long time-to-event. Even
with a long time-to-event, anchored sampling still takes a few windows per track at a known interval from a known event. Streaming sampling places its windows without reference to any event and produces a large share of negatives from a category absent from anchored sampling (\cref{sec:omits}).

\subsection{Four Kinds of Streaming Negative}
\label{sec:taxonomy}
The negative windows produced by streaming sampling fall into the four kinds
shown in \cref{fig:protocols}(b):
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{Genuine non-crossers}, who wait or walk along the pavement without crossing, and whose windows occur under both protocols.
  \item \textbf{Hard temporal negatives}, who will cross later than $H$ and
  match the positive windows that follow in person, clothing, street and
  camera, so that only the position of the cutoff makes the labels differ.
  \item \textbf{Already crossed} pedestrians, who are past their first crossing
  yet are labelled negative alongside genuine non-crossers, a labelling error
  built into the binary formulation.
  \item \textbf{Uninformative windows}, in which the pedestrian is hard to see, such as when occluded or far away.
\end{itemize}
The anchored protocol cannot produce the second and third kinds.

\section{What the Streaming Window Population Contains}
\label{sec:omits}
We use the standard PIE split, i.e., sets 01, 02 and 06 for training, 04 and 05
for validation, and 03 for testing. Streaming windows of $T = 20$ frames are extracted with a stride of $3$ and labelled with a horizon of $H = 32$ frames (about $1.07$\,s at $30$\,fps). Windows whose future is only partly observed are discarded instead of being labelled negative, because a crossing may have occurred in the unrecorded part. This leaves $\Ntrain$ training,
$20\,490$ validation and $\Ntest$ test windows, with positive rates of $2.9$,
$2.8$ and $3.1\%$, respectively, and every measurement in this section comes
directly from the PIE annotation files.

\subsection{What the Negatives Are Made Of}
Matching each window against the crossing annotation of its own track gives the
breakdown in \cref{tab:composition}. About one training window in four and two
test windows in five show a pedestrian who crosses, but later than the
horizon of \cref{eq:binlabel}, so that these windows outnumber the true
positives by $8.9{:}1$ in training and by $12.8{:}1$ in test.

The splits differ in composition. Hard temporal negatives make up $39.2\%$ of
the test split compared with $25.7\%$ of the training split, so the test split
has fewer easy negatives. Part of any drop in performance from training to test
may therefore come from the harder test split. Validation is the easiest split
($15.2\%$), so a threshold tuned on it may not suit the test split.
Keeping the test split out of threshold tuning does not remove this mismatch.

However, most of the hard temporal negatives lie far from the horizon, with
$68.7\%$ of them more than five seconds ahead. We refer to the windows whose
onset falls within the $64$ frames (about two seconds) after the horizon, i.e.,
between $1.1$\,s and $3.2$\,s, as the confusable band. With $4{,}186$ training
windows, $1.65$ times as many as the positive set, the band is large enough to train on. It is also the population on which a targeted method has to work.

\subsection{Why Not Predict Further Ahead}
\label{sec:horizon}
An obvious response to a $33.9{:}1$ imbalance would be to enlarge $H$.
Relabelling the same windows at longer horizons (\cref{tab:horizon}) does lower the ratio. At five seconds it falls to $5.8{:}1$, and the confusable band shrinks from $1.65$ to $0.26$ times the positive set. At ten seconds the
ratio reaches $2.4{:}1$, close to the anchored $2.5{:}1$. The match is a
coincidence, but it shows how much of the anchored ratio comes from a sampling
choice. We
nevertheless keep $H = 32$. A longer horizon requires more observed future per window. As a result, we lose $26\%$ of the windows at five seconds and $44\%$ at ten,
and the short tracks of pedestrians who appear suddenly or are soon occluded
lose their windows first. The confusion also moves instead of disappearing. Windows that were hard temporal negatives at one second become positives at five. The model has no better way than before to tell a window $4.9$\,s before onset from one $5.1$\,s before onset, yet it now has to answer yes on the first, turning false alarms into misses. Finally, the horizon determines what
the model has to detect. About one second before onset the cue is the body starting to move, e.g., a shift of weight or a lifted foot. Five seconds ahead, only the person's heading relative to the road is visible, so a longer horizon is a different task.

\section{Cross-Protocol Evaluation}
\label{sec:gap}

\subsection{Model and Training Setup}
All runs use the same pixel-free pose and kinematics model. Each frame is a
$58$-dimensional vector of $49$ pose measurements from $23$ whole-body
keypoints~\cite{jin2020coco} and $9$ channels for bounding-box position and
size, their velocities and the ego-vehicle speed, each standardised with
training-set statistics and clipped at $\pm 5$ standard deviations. Two temporal
convolutions, a two-layer gated recurrent unit and multi-head self-attention
encode the $20$-frame window at model dimension $128$ (about $0.66$\,M parameters). A per-frame crossing head, pooled over frames by log-sum-exp, gives the binary output. All runs share a learning rate of $10^{-4}$, a weight
decay of $10^{-5}$, a batch size of $32$ and a warmup-then-cosine schedule over
at most $30$ epochs. A weighted sampler raises the share of crossing windows
among the training draws. In addition, the training windows are stored in a globally shuffled order so that each data chunk carries the global class mix. The checkpoint with the highest validation AUC is kept, training stops after $15$ epochs without improvement, and each configuration is run with seeds $42$, $43$ and $44$.

\subsection{The Cross-Protocol Matrix}
Each model is trained under one protocol and evaluated under both, with
thresholds selected on the validation set and applied unchanged to the test set
(sweeps on the test split serve as a diagnostic only). \cref{tab:matrix}
reports the matrix for a model trained on the crossing task alone (Model~B) and
one trained on all three annotation tasks (Model~A).

While the anchored-to-anchored scores lie in the range reported for the standard
benchmark~\cite{kotseruba2021benchmark}, the main finding is in the
off-diagonal entries. Over three seeds, the crossing-only model trained under
the anchored protocol reaches $0.889 \pm 0.007$ AUC on the anchored test set
but $0.516 \pm 0.010$ on the streaming one, close to chance. The three-task model shows the same collapse (from $0.869$ to $0.511$), which rules out interference between the task heads as the explanation.

\subsection{Splitting the Deployment Gap}
We decompose the gap into the part that threshold re-tuning recovers and the
remainder, using F1 scores on the streaming test set:
\begin{align}
  \Gtot   &= \mathrm{F1}^{\text{tuned}}_{\text{str}\to\text{str}}
             - \mathrm{F1}^{\text{raw}}_{\text{anc}\to\text{str}}, \label{eq:gtot}\\
  \Gprior &= \mathrm{F1}^{\text{tuned}}_{\text{anc}\to\text{str}}
             - \mathrm{F1}^{\text{raw}}_{\text{anc}\to\text{str}}, \label{eq:gprior}\\
  \Ghard  &= \mathrm{F1}^{\text{tuned}}_{\text{str}\to\text{str}}
             - \mathrm{F1}^{\text{tuned}}_{\text{anc}\to\text{str}}, \label{eq:ghard}
\end{align}
so that $\Gtot = \Gprior + \Ghard$ by construction. Here $\Gprior$ measures the
gain from re-choosing the operating point at the streaming base rate, i.e., the
part of the gap that a change of threshold can close. We attribute the
remainder, $\Ghard$, to the hard temporal negatives that anchored training never
sees. The decomposition is the same for both models. For Model~B, averaged over
three seeds, $\Gtot = +0.180 \pm 0.006$, $\Gprior = 0.000 \pm 0.001$ and
$\Ghard = +0.180 \pm 0.006$. For Model~A the values are $+0.203$, $+0.003$
and $+0.200$. In both cases re-tuning the threshold recovers almost none of the gap.

The AUC values explain this result. A ranking that has degraded to $0.516$,
barely better than a random ordering, leaves no good threshold to find, because
re-tuning a threshold only moves the decision boundary along the existing
ranking. By the same argument, any method whose main effect is to shift the
operating point, such as class re-weighting or focal
loss~\cite{lin2017focal,cui2019classbalanced}, should run into the same limit.
A test of this prediction is planned in \cref{sec:ablations}.

\subsection{Anchored Training Learns Who Crosses but Not When}
\label{sec:whowhen}
A positive streaming label combines two conditions: the pedestrian will cross,
and the crossing starts within $H$. We score the two separately on the streaming test set (\cref{tab:whowhen}). The \emph{who} score averages each pedestrian's window scores up to the crossing and ranks crossers against pedestrians who never cross. The \emph{when} score, computed only on windows whose crossing still lies ahead, ranks onsets within $H$ against later ones.

a later crossing, it scores the later one higher in $59\%$ of the pairs. Its
separability against never-crossers rises from $0.60$ for onsets within one
second to $0.71$ for onsets more than ten seconds away, the opposite of every streaming-trained model (\cref{sec:discussion}). At $205$ alarms per hour it detects $15.1\%$ of the crossing pedestrians, about $21$ seconds before onset on average. The anchored protocol explains this behaviour, since it labels whole
tracks (\cref{sec:protocols}) and presents crossing pedestrians only close to
the onset, leaving the model without a training signal that separates an
imminent crossing from a distant one.

Combining the anchored model's scores with those of a streaming model after
training does not improve detection. In a
test specified in advance, the anchored-trained model's score is multiplied
window by window with that of the streaming binary baseline and judged by the
criterion of \cref{sec:evalprotocol} over three seeds, pairing runs with the
same seed. The product detects $42.3\%$ of crossing pedestrians at $205$ alarms
per hour and $23.1\%$ at $41$, against $42.3\%$ and $22.9\%$ for the baseline
alone (\cref{sec:detection}), with its AUC dropping to $0.757$. A combination
fitted on validation data reproduces the baseline. Since the anchored model's
\emph{who} signal adds no detections in these combinations, our method changes
the training itself (\cref{sec:method}).

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
Four issues limit these results and those in \cref{sec:experiments}.

\textbf{Training set size.} The anchored training set contains about $4.9$k
windows, compared with $88$k for the streaming set. Since $\Ghard$ thus
confounds the training protocol with training set size, we describe these
results as consistent with a gap caused by hard temporal negatives, without
claiming that they establish one, and we have not yet run a matched-size control.

\textbf{Instability of F1.} With one positive in $34$ windows, F1 changes
noticeably when only a few samples cross the threshold. The AUC drop from about $0.87$ to about $0.52$ in both models is the more reliable measurement. Six of the $40$ tuned thresholds also lie at the lower edge of the
validation sweep ($0.01$), two of them in the seed-$42$ gap decomposition. A finer sweep could raise those tuned F1 values slightly.

\textbf{Early overfitting.} In $19$ of the $20$ runs, validation AUC peaks
within the first eight epochs and then declines as the training loss keeps falling. The checkpoint at the peak is the one reported. The horizon read-out of the onset configurations (\cref{sec:method}) narrows in the same way, with the gap between its 5th and
95th percentiles on validation shrinking to near zero by the last epoch, against $0.13$ to $0.57$ at the selected checkpoints of the pure, hedged and censored configurations. The spread across seeds includes
this selection step.

\textbf{Order of decisions.} We chose the detection metric after seeing the
window-level results. The metric follows from the deployment requirement and
from quickest change detection~\cite{page1954cusum} and was fixed in
\cref{sec:evalprotocol} before the seed replicates were run, but readers should
keep this order in mind.

\section{Onset Timing Under Right-Censoring}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Where the Binary Label Goes Wrong}
The binary label of \cref{eq:binlabel} is wrong by construction near the cutoff and where the footage ends. Near the cutoff, a window whose crossing begins $H + 5$ frames
after its last frame is labelled negative and grouped with pedestrians who
never cross, although it looks almost identical to a window at $H - 5$ frames,
which is labelled positive. Only the position of the cutoff separates the two
labels, so the task is ill-posed there. Where the footage ends early,
a window with fewer than $H$ observed future frames and no crossing in them fits
either label. Labelling it negative relies on an interval that nobody observed.
Discarding it, as in \cref{sec:omits}, throws away its observed frames and the
one fact known about its future: no crossing began before the footage ended. Both situations are typical
of a right-censored time-to-event dataset forced into binary classification.

Besides the binary label, each streaming window is annotated with the number of
frames $o$ between its last observed frame and the next crossing onset (set to
$-1$ if the remaining footage lacks an onset), the number of future frames
$f$ available after the window, and a flag $c$ indicating whether the track
contains a crossing at any point. In survival-analysis terms, $o$ is the event
time and $f$ the censoring time. The flag $c$ matters for windows without an
onset in the remaining footage, where it separates pedestrians who crossed
earlier in the track from those whose observation is censored.

\subsection{Discrete-Time Hazard}
The next $L$ frames are divided into $K = L/w$ bins of width $w$. We write $h_k$
for the hazard of bin $k$, i.e., the probability that a first crossing begins
in that bin given that none has begun earlier. The model outputs $K$
logits from the pooled representation. The probability of an onset within a
horizon spanning $K_H = H/w$ bins then follows from the survival product
\begin{equation}
  P(\text{onset} \le H) = 1 - \prod_{k=0}^{K_H - 1}\left(1 - h_k\right).
  \label{eq:readout}
\end{equation}
Since \cref{eq:readout} answers the question posed by \cref{eq:binlabel}, the
timing model reports the same quantity as the binary baselines and is evaluated
with the same metric.

\subsection{Supervision When the Future Is Only Partly Seen}
This annotation determines a target $y_k$ and a mask $m_k$ for each bin, as
illustrated in \cref{fig:cases}.
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{Onset within range} ($0 \le o < L$). With $e = \lfloor o/w
  \rfloor$, bins $0$ to $e-1$ are supervised as $0$ and bin $e$ as $1$, and
  later bins are masked, as they are uninformative about a first crossing.
  \item \textbf{Onset beyond range} ($o \ge L$). All $K$ bins are supervised as
  $0$, since the footage shows the crossing falling outside the look-ahead.
  \item \textbf{Censored} ($o < 0$, with some future seen). The bins covered by
  $f$ are supervised as $0$ and the rest are masked, a case the binary label
  cannot express.
  \item \textbf{Already crossed} ($o < 0$ and $c$). The window is dropped, as
  the pedestrian is past the first crossing, a case the binary label mislabels
  as negative.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\columnwidth]{fig4_hazard_cases.pdf}
  \caption{Supervision cases of the hazard target, in which shaded bins
  contribute to the loss, dashed bins are masked with zero gradient, and the
  case~4 window leaves the risk set.}
  \label{fig:cases}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Training Objective}
The training loss is a masked binary cross-entropy over the bins:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{onset}} = -\sum_{k=0}^{K-1} m_k
    \Big[\, y_k \log h_k + (1 - y_k)\log(1 - h_k) \,\Big].
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
The mask gives zero gradient to every bin after the onset or beyond the end of
the footage, instead of a weak push towards zero, and a unit test checks this with
automatic differentiation. The implementation also enforces
two conditions on the horizons. The look-ahead must be strictly longer than the
reported horizon ($L > H$), because a look-ahead no wider than $H$ would again
assign a flat negative label to a crossing at $H + 5$. The read-out horizon
must also match that of the binary baselines, or \cref{eq:readout} would
silently answer a different question under the same metric name. A test checks this by reproducing the generator's binary label from the timing
read-out over a full track.

\subsection{Choosing the Look-Ahead and Bin Width}
The reported horizon remains $H = 32$ frames (\cref{sec:horizon}). The look-ahead $L$ is sized against the composition measured in \cref{sec:omits}.
Since the confusable band ends $64$ frames after $H$, a look-ahead of $L = 96$
frames ($3.2$\,s) covers the whole band, against $47\%$ of it at $L = 60$. It also gives a positive ``crossing begins here'' signal to $6{,}716$ training windows instead of the $2{,}530$ at $L = H$. A wider look-ahead would bring small gains,
because two thirds of the hard temporal negatives lie more than five seconds
ahead. The bin width $w = 4$ gives $K = 24$ bins, $8$ of them inside the
reported horizon, and makes positives about four times denser per bin at the
cost of $133$\,ms of timing resolution. Both settings affect only the training.

\subsection{Configurations of the Objective}
Changing the loss weights alone gives three configurations. In the
\emph{auxiliary} configuration the binary head produces the reported output and
the hazard term acts as a regulariser. In the \emph{pure} configuration the hazard is the only objective and \cref{eq:readout} gives the reported output. The \emph{hedged} configuration adds a term on the read-out itself,
because under \emph{pure} the optimised quantity differs from the reported one.
Since \cref{eq:loss} sums over the observed bins, a fully observed window
contributes about $17$ nats at initialisation, against $5.5$ for a window with
only the $32$ future frames that generation guarantees. The hazard term is thus scaled down when it is used alongside the binary objective.

\textbf{Collapse of the hazard head.} With $K$ bins, each bin sees a positive
only about $2.9\%/K$ of the time, and the head can lower the loss by predicting
$h \approx 0$ everywhere. A narrow spike of the read-out at the base rate
across validation windows reveals this failure, and $w = 4$ is the mitigation applied.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Detection Rate at a Fixed Alarm Budget Is the Primary Metric}
\label{sec:evalprotocol}
We evaluate detection rate, lead time and false-alarm rate together, as a
detector on a vehicle is judged on all three. Each window of the streaming test
split yields one decision, giving \decisionsPerHour{} decisions per hour at
stride $3$ and $30$\,fps. A crossing pedestrian counts as detected if any window
whose crossing still lies ahead scores above the threshold, and the lead time
is the largest such offset, i.e., the earliest warning the model would have
given. Alarms raised after the person has stepped out are ignored. Every window of the \Nnoncrossers{} never-crossing pedestrians (against \Ncrossers{} crossing ones) is an opportunity for a false alarm. Sweeping the threshold traces detection rate against alarm rate, which we
read at budgets of $1200$, $460$, $205$, $95$ and $41$ alarms per hour. Each
budget fixes the lowest threshold whose realised alarm rate stays within it, so that configurations are compared at the same alarm budget. The
accounting follows quickest change detection~\cite{page1954cusum}, which has
measured detection delay against the time between false alarms since the
1950s. We use its accounting but not its cumulative-sum (CUSUM) detector, since CUSUM assumes a persistent shift and the approach to a crossing is a brief change.

\textbf{Accounting rules.} \texttt{per\_window} accounting counts every alarming window. Under \texttt{per\_track} accounting, a never-crossing pedestrian who triggers any alarm counts as a single false alarm, as a driver would experience it. Both rules divide by the same exposure time. Since they disagree at loose budgets, every reported number names its rule.

\textbf{Pre-registration.} The budgets, accounting rules, metrics and success
criterion were fixed before the seed replicates were run. The criterion
requires the mean detection rate of the pure hazard configuration to exceed that
of the binary baseline by more than the sum of their standard deviations at
three or more of the five budgets, and any smaller difference is reported as
inconclusive. AUC and tuned F1 are reported alongside (\cref{sec:windowtie}).

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}
\textbf{Binary baseline.} The baseline is trained on the streaming protocol with
a single crossing head and the standard cross-entropy objective at $H = 32$. It
shares the architecture, optimiser, sampler and schedule of the onset
configurations, differs from them only in the loss, and runs on the same three
seeds as the pure hazard configuration.

\textbf{Onset configurations.} The auxiliary, pure and hedged weightings of \cref{sec:method} are evaluated together with a fourth configuration that adds the
\seedconf{7\,470} right-censored training windows which the generator would
otherwise discard. The auxiliary configuration reports its binary head. The others report $P(\text{onset} \le 32)$ through \cref{eq:readout}. All rows thus answer the same question at the same horizon.

\textbf{Published methods.} Published crossing-intention methods were designed for the anchored protocol. Retraining them would mainly measure how well we
reimplemented them. We leave them out, as the binary baseline isolates the one
variable this paper changes: the training objective.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{How Far Ahead Crossing Is Predictable at All}
Consider a pedestrian who walks along the pavement like everyone else and then
turns suddenly into the road. Three seconds earlier, the person may not have decided yet, or the decision may not yet show in their body. No choice of input, architecture or loss lets a model predict such a crossing, and an unknown share of the confusable band is of this kind. A realistic goal is a model that is
confident where the evidence exists and uncertain where it is missing, which is
a question of ranking and calibration and matches the failure we measured.

The position of this limit can be measured by scoring windows at a given time to
onset against windows of pedestrians who never cross. For the binary and pure hazard objectives, AUC is $0.81$ to $0.82$ within the first second,
$0.72$ to $0.73$ between two and three seconds, $0.64$ to $0.66$ from five to
ten seconds, and $0.52$ to $0.54$, i.e., chance level, beyond ten seconds. This drop is much larger than the seed spread, which stays below $0.03$ in each of these time ranges. Both objectives follow the same curve to within $0.02$. The anchored-trained model, in contrast, shows the reverse profile (\cref{sec:whowhen}).
Crossing is thus predictable from this input for roughly ten seconds at most, a
result that suggests a limit for any method using the same input on PIE and
supports the short horizon chosen in \cref{sec:horizon}. We have not found this
measurement reported elsewhere in the crossing-intention literature, although
it requires only one evaluation pass over an existing model.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:ablations}
Besides the issues in \cref{sec:threats}, all results come from PIE, recorded in one city in daylight and clear weather. Replication on JAAD would show whether the effect is specific to this dataset. The
streaming-trained entries also rest on checkpoints selected at an early
validation peak, which may not show the best these models can do. We have not
yet run several planned ablations: varying the look-ahead over $L \in \{60, 96, 150\}$ and the
bin width over $w \in \{4, 8\}$ to test the sizing argument of
\cref{sec:method}, focal and class-balanced losses on the binary objective to
separate our claim from a claim about class imbalance, and a comparison of
frame-pooling rules.

\subsection{What This Means for Evaluation Practice}
If $\Ghard \approx \Gtot$ holds more widely, a model's anchored score says
little about how it behaves on a stream, and the standard protocol has left
the safety-critical case out of training and testing. Whether this holds is
cheap to check, since the streaming sampler only relabels existing annotations. Recent
work revisiting the definitions and metrics used on PIE and
JAAD~\cite{rasouli2024diving} shows that the field already questions the
standard evaluation. Our results suggest that the sampling protocol deserves part of that attention. At $205$ alarms per hour,
three seeds of our binary objective also detect between $38.5\%$ and $44.4\%$ of
the crossing pedestrians, a range as wide as the largest gap we measured between
objectives. Streaming evaluations on PIE should always report the run-to-run spread, and a difference between methods
should exceed this spread before it counts as evidence.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The event-anchored protocol and the streaming condition contain different
negatives and pose different tasks. We measured what the anchored protocol leaves out: a quarter of the
streaming training windows and two fifths of the test windows show pedestrians
who cross later. A model trained under the anchored protocol falls close to
chance ranking on the streaming test set, and threshold re-tuning recovers
almost none of the loss. Because part of the difficulty lies in the
labelling, we proposed onset timing under right-censoring. A window whose future footage runs out then counts as censored and a pedestrian who has already crossed leaves the risk set. The reported horizon probability remains directly comparable with the binary baselines.

Whether the reformulation detects crossings better remains open. Measured against a criterion fixed before the runs, with three seeds of each objective and detection rate at matched false-alarm budgets, it is indistinguishable from binary training. The practical finding concerns the run-to-run spread, which is
as wide as the largest gap between objectives: at a $33.9{:}1$ base rate, a claim about an objective has to be supported by several runs, whether it rests on window-level scores or on detection curves.
