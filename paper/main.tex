\begin{abstract}
Pedestrian crossing prediction is usually benchmarked with an event-anchored
protocol, which samples a few windows per pedestrian one to two seconds before
an annotated crossing. However, a vehicle-mounted detector operates
continuously and has to decide at every moment whether a crossing is about to
begin. On the Pedestrian Intention Estimation (PIE) dataset, the anchored
protocol yields approximately $2.5$ negative windows per positive, compared
with $33.9$ for streaming sampling. In the streaming test set, $39\%$ of
windows show a pedestrian crossing only after the one-second horizon, a type
of negative absent from the anchored protocol. Our model, trained under the
anchored protocol, drops from $0.89$ to $0.52$ area under the ROC
curve (AUC) on streaming data, and threshold re-tuning recovers almost none of
this loss. The model still separates crossing from non-crossing pedestrians
($0.80$ AUC) but ranks imminent crossings below distant ones. We then predict
the onset time of a crossing and treat windows whose footage ends early as
censored observations instead of negatives. The probability of a crossing
within the original horizon is still reported. Across three training seeds
per method, the detection rates of the two formulations at fixed false-alarm
rates lie within the seed-to-seed spread.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A pedestrian-crossing detector on a moving vehicle works as a streaming system. It judges at every moment, for every pedestrian in view, whether someone is about to step into the road, even though crossings are rare events. The protocol used to evaluate such systems asks a narrower question.
This protocol came with the Pedestrian Intention Estimation (PIE) and Joint Attention in Autonomous Driving (JAAD) datasets~\cite{rasouli2019pie,rasouli2017jaad}, and a later evaluation suite~\cite{kotseruba2021benchmark} made it standard. It takes a few labelled windows from each pedestrian track, with their last frames one to two seconds before the crossing event, where observation stops. The resulting class ratio
is low, about $2.5$ negatives per positive ($2.5{:}1$), and reported scores are high.

The two settings differ in more than difficulty. Because the anchored protocol
places its windows relative to a known event, it skips the earlier windows of a crossing pedestrian's track, in which the person is still walking
along the pavement (\cref{fig:protocols}). Under a short horizon these windows are negatives that look almost identical to the positives following them. A deployed detector has to stay silent on them. The protocol leaves these windows out of both training and testing, so models never see them in either. This paper measures that omission and its cost.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{fig1_protocols.pdf}
  \caption{The two sampling protocols on one pedestrian track, with (a)~event-anchored windows ending a known interval before a known crossing onset and (b)~streaming windows covering the whole track, including negatives of the same pedestrian before the onset (drawn further apart than in practice, where windows are $20$ frames long and start every $3$ frames).}
  \label{fig:protocols}
\end{figure*}

This paper makes five contributions.
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{Protocol analysis.} The anchored class ratio measures how many
  pedestrians eventually cross, and the streaming ratio measures how often a
  crossing is about to begin (\cref{sec:protocols}).
  \item \textbf{Composition of the streaming negatives.} The PIE training and
  test splits contain different shares of negatives from pedestrians who cross
  later, and most of these lie far from the horizon (\cref{sec:omits}).
  \item \textbf{Cross-protocol diagnosis.} Threshold re-tuning recovers
  almost none of the anchored-to-streaming gap, and the anchored-trained model also identifies who crosses but orders the timing of their crossings in
  reverse (\cref{sec:gap}).
  \item \textbf{Onset timing under right-censoring.} A discrete-time hazard
  gives, for each future interval, the probability that a crossing starts there
  given that none has started earlier. The loss ignores unobserved intervals. Because the model still reports the probability of a crossing within the original horizon, it stays comparable with binary baselines (\cref{sec:method}).
  \item \textbf{Evaluation at a $33.9{:}1$ base rate.} The \FmeanGap{} gap
  between the two objectives' mean F1 barely exceeds the sum of their standard deviations, and smoothing the scores along each track moves AUC by about as much as the objectives differ. We also compare detection rate at matched false-alarm budgets, as in quickest change detection~\cite{page1954cusum}. By a criterion fixed in advance, the objectives tie on this measure too, and the binary baseline warns earlier
  (\cref{sec:experiments}).
\end{enumerate}
Streaming evaluation itself is, however, well established in online action detection~\cite{degeest2016oad,shou2018odas,gao2019startnet}. Our claim is only that the crossing-intention literature has not adopted it, that this has a measurable cost, and that a timing formulation addresses the labelling
problem directly.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Crossing Intention Estimation on PIE and JAAD}
PIE~\cite{rasouli2019pie} and JAAD~\cite{rasouli2017jaad} are the standard datasets, and most later work uses the evaluation protocol of Kotseruba \emph{et al.}~\cite{kotseruba2021benchmark} largely unchanged, with per-track binary
labels, observation clipped at the crossing point and a time-to-event interval
of one to two seconds. Azarmi \emph{et al.}~\cite{azarmi2024feature} split PIE into context sets and report a strong dependence on ego-vehicle speed. In addition, Rasouli and Kotseruba~\cite{rasouli2024diving} revise the task definitions and add new types of metric. Both keep the window population of the anchored protocol, the population this paper questions. Yao \emph{et
al.}~\cite{yao2021coupling} come closest to our work. They report results for an ``event-to-crossing'' setting, with sequences from the one to two seconds before the crossing, and for an ``original data'' setting that uses the whole track, and note that the first contains more changes in action. We instead label a window by whether an onset falls within a fixed horizon and study the early windows that the event-to-crossing setting omits.

\subsection{Online Action Detection}
Online action detection labels each frame of a stream using present and past
evidence only~\cite{degeest2016oad}. Its onset-focused variant, action start
detection~\cite{shou2018odas,gao2019startnet}, has to signal a beginning as
early as possible in untrimmed video dominated by background frames, mostly
with transformer-based
methods~\cite{wang2021oadtr,xu2021lstr,zhao2022testra,wang2023mat} and with
evaluation tools such as calibrated and point-level average precision. Shou
\emph{et al.}~\cite{shou2018odas} note that a start window shares scene and
objects with the preceding frames, which places its features closer to the background than to the action that follows. A window taken just before someone
steps off the kerb also resembles that person waiting more than it
resembles a crossing. Pedestrian crossing is often cited as a motivating
example in this literature, although its evaluations are run on television, sports and ego-vehicle manoeuvre data.

\subsection{Time-to-Event Modelling}
Survival analysis models the time until an event when some observations end
before the event occurs~\cite{kaplan1958nonparametric,cox1972regression}. The per-interval hazard with a masked binary likelihood used in \cref{sec:method} is standard there and has been carried over to neural estimators~\cite{lee2018deephit}. Kalatian and
Farooq~\cite{kalatian2019deepwait} apply survival modelling to pedestrian
waiting times at unsignalised crossings, using a neural Cox model trained on
virtual-reality participant data, which is a different setting and quantity
from onset detection in in-vehicle video. A targeted but non-exhaustive search
found no prior work applying censored time-to-event modelling to crossing onset
under the PIE protocol. The contribution here is the observation that
streaming crossing windows already form a right-censored dataset, and that
binary labelling throws away the censoring information.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:protocols}

\subsection{Event-Anchored Sampling}
We consider a pedestrian track with annotated frames $1,\dots,n$ and denote by
$\tau$ the frame at which a crossing pedestrian begins to cross. In the
anchored protocol, observation is stopped at $\tau$ (or at the last visible
frame for non-crossing pedestrians). A small number of windows of length $T$
is then extracted from each track, with the final frame of each window in a
fixed interval before $\tau$, typically $30$ to $60$ frames. Labels are
assigned at the track level, indicating whether the pedestrian crosses at all.
The resulting proportion of positives reflects the fraction of pedestrians
that eventually cross, roughly $2.5{:}1$ on PIE.

\subsection{Streaming Sampling}
In the streaming protocol, we place windows at every stride-$s$ position along
the entire track and label the window ending at frame $t$ as
\begin{equation}
  y_t = \mathbf{1}\left[\, \exists\ \text{onset in } (t,\, t+H] \,\right],
  \label{eq:binlabel}
\end{equation}
where $H$ denotes the anticipation horizon. We discard windows in which a
crossing is already under way, since no onset is left to predict. Here, the
proportion of positives is the share of observed moments at which a crossing
is imminent: $2.9\%$ of the windows on PIE (a ratio of $33.9{:}1$).

In the anchored protocol, the same small number of windows is taken from each
track, and the anchored ratio of $2.5{:}1$ is a balance across tracks. The
streaming ratio of $33.9{:}1$, in contrast, is a rate over time. The two
ratios thus estimate different quantities. Neither setting is a harder or
easier version of the other. Streaming sampling is also
not equivalent to anchored sampling with a long time-to-event, which would
still take a few windows per track at a known interval from a known event. In
streaming sampling, the placement of windows is independent of any event,
producing a large number of negatives of a kind absent from anchored sampling
(\cref{sec:omits}).

\subsection{Four Kinds of Streaming Negative}
\label{sec:taxonomy}
We divide streaming negatives into the four kinds of
\cref{fig:protocols}(b):
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{Genuine non-crossers}, i.e., pedestrians waiting or walking
  along the pavement without crossing, seen in both protocols.
  \item \textbf{Hard temporal negatives} of pedestrians crossing later than
  $H$. They show the same person, clothing, street and camera as the positive
  windows that follow. Only the position of the cutoff gives them a different
  label.
  \item \textbf{Already crossed} pedestrians, past their first crossing but
  wrongly labelled negative alongside genuine non-crossers.
  \item \textbf{Uninformative windows}, in which the pedestrian is difficult to
  see, e.g., when occluded or far away.
\end{itemize}
The anchored protocol cannot produce kinds two and three.

\section{Composition of the Streaming Windows}
\label{sec:omits}
Our experiments follow the standard PIE split, using sets 01, 02 and 06 for
training, 04 and 05 for validation, and 03 for testing. Streaming windows of
$T = 20$ frames are extracted with a stride of $3$ and labelled using a
horizon of $H = 32$ frames (approximately $1.07$\,s at $30$\,fps). Windows
with a partly observed future are discarded instead of being labelled
negative, since a crossing may have occurred in the unrecorded part. After
this filtering step, $\Ntrain$ training, $20\,490$ validation and $\Ntest$
test windows remain, with positive rates of $2.9$, $2.8$ and $3.1\%$,
respectively. All measurements in this section are computed directly from the
PIE annotation files.

\subsection{Types of Negative Window}
To obtain the breakdown in \cref{tab:composition}, we compared each window
with the crossing annotation of its own track. In about one training window in
four and two test windows in five, the pedestrian crosses, but later than the
horizon of \cref{eq:binlabel}. These hard temporal negatives outnumber the
true positives by $8.9{:}1$ in training and by $12.8{:}1$ in test.

The composition also differs between the splits. Hard temporal negatives
account for $39.2\%$ of the test windows and $25.7\%$ of the training windows,
leaving fewer easy negatives for testing. Part of any performance drop from
training to test may come from this difference in difficulty. Threshold
selection may be affected as well: the validation split is the easiest
($15.2\%$), and thresholds tuned on it may not suit the test split, even
though the test data are excluded from tuning.

However, most of the hard temporal negatives lie far from the horizon: for
$68.7\%$ of them, the onset is more than five seconds ahead. We define the
confusable band as the set of windows with an onset in the $64$ frames
(approximately two seconds) after the horizon, i.e., between $1.1$\,s and
$3.2$\,s. The band has $4{,}186$ training windows, $1.65$ times the number of
positives. It is large enough for training and is the population a targeted
method must handle.

\subsection{Choice of Horizon}
\label{sec:horizon}
An obvious way of reducing the $33.9{:}1$ imbalance would be to enlarge $H$.
If the same windows are relabelled at longer horizons (\cref{tab:horizon}),
the ratio falls to $5.8{:}1$ at five seconds and the confusable band shrinks
from $1.65$ to $0.26$ times the size of the positive set. At ten seconds, the
ratio reaches $2.4{:}1$, close to the anchored $2.5{:}1$. This agreement is a
coincidence, but it indicates how much of the anchored ratio results from a
sampling choice.

We nevertheless keep $H = 32$, for three reasons. First, longer horizons
require more observed future per window, which removes $26\%$ of the windows
at five seconds and $44\%$ at ten, starting with the short tracks of
pedestrians who appear suddenly or are soon occluded. Second, hard temporal negatives at one
second become positives at five, and the confusion moves to the new cutoff.
The model has no better means than before of distinguishing a window $4.9$\,s
before onset from one at $5.1$\,s, yet it is now required to answer yes on
the first, turning false alarms into misses. Third, a longer horizon
corresponds to a different task. Approximately one second before onset, the
cue is the beginning of body movement, e.g., a shift of weight or a lifted
foot, whereas five seconds ahead only the heading of the person relative to
the road is visible.

\section{Cross-Protocol Evaluation}
\label{sec:gap}

\subsection{Model and Training Setup}
Our pixel-free pose and kinematics model is used in all runs. Each frame is a
$58$-dimensional vector of $49$ pose measurements from $23$ whole-body
keypoints~\cite{jin2020coco} and $9$ channels for bounding-box position and
size, their velocities and the ego-vehicle speed. All channels are
standardised using training-set statistics and clipped at $\pm 5$ standard
deviations. The $20$-frame window is encoded by two temporal convolutions, a
two-layer gated recurrent unit and multi-head self-attention at a model
dimension of $128$ (approximately $0.66$\,M parameters). The binary output is
produced by a per-frame crossing head with log-sum-exp pooling over frames. We
train with a learning rate of $10^{-4}$,
a weight decay of $10^{-5}$, a batch size of $32$ and a warmup-then-cosine
schedule over at most $30$ epochs. A weighted sampler increases the proportion
of crossing windows among the training samples. To give each data chunk the
global class distribution, we store the training windows in a globally
shuffled order. We select the checkpoint with the highest validation AUC and
stop training after $15$ epochs without improvement. Each configuration was
run with seeds $42$, $43$ and $44$.

are selected on the validation set and applied unchanged to the test set.
Sweeps on the test split serve only as a diagnostic. \cref{tab:matrix} reports the results of a model trained on the
crossing task alone (Model~B) and of one trained on all three annotation
tasks (Model~A).

The anchored-to-anchored scores fall within the range reported for the
standard benchmark~\cite{kotseruba2021benchmark}. Our main finding concerns
the off-diagonal entries. Over three seeds, the crossing-only model trained
under the anchored protocol reaches $0.889 \pm 0.007$ AUC on the anchored test
set but $0.516 \pm 0.010$ on the streaming test set, close to chance level. The three-task model collapses in the same way (from $0.869$ to
$0.511$), ruling out interference between the task heads as an explanation.

\subsection{Splitting the Deployment Gap}
We decompose the gap into the part recovered by threshold re-tuning and the
remainder, using streaming test F1 scores:
\begin{align}
  \Gtot   &= \mathrm{F1}^{\text{tuned}}_{\text{str}\to\text{str}}
             - \mathrm{F1}^{\text{raw}}_{\text{anc}\to\text{str}}, \label{eq:gtot}\\
  \Gprior &= \mathrm{F1}^{\text{tuned}}_{\text{anc}\to\text{str}}
             - \mathrm{F1}^{\text{raw}}_{\text{anc}\to\text{str}}, \label{eq:gprior}\\
  \Ghard  &= \mathrm{F1}^{\text{tuned}}_{\text{str}\to\text{str}}
             - \mathrm{F1}^{\text{tuned}}_{\text{anc}\to\text{str}}, \label{eq:ghard}
\end{align}
so that $\Gtot = \Gprior + \Ghard$ holds by construction. Here, $\Gprior$
measures the gain from re-selecting the operating point at the streaming base
rate, i.e., the part of the gap that can be closed by adjusting the threshold.
We attribute the remainder, $\Ghard$, to the hard temporal negatives absent
from anchored training. Averaged over three seeds, Model~B gives
$\Gtot = +0.180 \pm 0.006$, $\Gprior = 0.000 \pm 0.001$ and
$\Ghard = +0.180 \pm 0.006$. The corresponding values for Model~A are
$+0.203$, $+0.003$ and $+0.200$. In both cases, threshold re-tuning recovers
almost none of the gap.

The reason can be seen in the AUC values. Re-tuning a threshold only moves the
decision boundary along the existing ranking. A ranking that has degraded to
$0.516$, barely better than a random ordering, leaves no good threshold to
find. By the same argument, any method whose main effect is a shift of the
operating point, such as class re-weighting or focal
loss~\cite{lin2017focal,cui2019classbalanced}, should face the same limit.
We plan to test this prediction (\cref{sec:ablations}).

\subsection{Who Crosses and When}
\label{sec:whowhen}
A streaming window is positive if the pedestrian crosses and the crossing
starts no later than $H$ frames after the window. These two parts of the label
are scored separately on the streaming test set (\cref{tab:whowhen}). The
\emph{who} score is computed per pedestrian: window scores are averaged up to
the crossing, and crossers are ranked against never-crossers. The \emph{when}
score is computed on the windows before a crossing and ranks onsets inside $H$
against later onsets.

\end{table}

Compared with either streaming model, the anchored-trained model identifies
crossers better but orders their windows backwards in time. A \emph{when} AUC
of $0.414$ means that, in pairs consisting of one window before an imminent
crossing and one before a later crossing, the later window receives the higher
score in $59\%$ of cases. Its separation from
never-crossers increases from $0.60$ for onsets within one second to $0.71$
for onsets more than ten seconds away, opposite to the behaviour of every
streaming-trained model (\cref{sec:discussion}). At $205$ alarms per hour, the
model detects $15.1\%$ of the crossing pedestrians, on average approximately
$21$ seconds before onset. This behaviour follows from the anchored protocol,
which assigns labels to whole tracks (\cref{sec:protocols}) and presents
crossing pedestrians only close to the onset. During training, the model thus
receives no signal separating an imminent crossing from a distant one.

Detection did not improve when the scores of the anchored model were combined
with those of a streaming model after training. In a test specified in advance, we
multiplied the score of the anchored-trained model window by window with that
of the streaming binary baseline and judged the product by the criterion of
\cref{sec:evalprotocol} over three seeds, pairing runs with the same seed. The
product detects $42.3\%$ of crossing pedestrians at $205$ alarms per hour and
$23.1\%$ at $41$, compared with $42.3\%$ and $22.9\%$ for the baseline alone
(\cref{sec:detection}), and its AUC drops to $0.757$. A combination fitted on
validation data reproduces the baseline. Our method changes the training
itself instead (\cref{sec:method}), since the anchored \emph{who} signal adds
no detections here.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
The following four issues apply to the results of this section and of
\cref{sec:experiments}.

\textbf{Training set size.} The anchored training set contains about $4.9$k
windows, compared with $88$k for the streaming set. Because $\Ghard$ thus
confounds the training protocol with training set size, we describe these
results as consistent with a gap caused by hard temporal negatives, without
claiming that they establish one. We have not yet run a matched-size control.

\textbf{Instability of F1.} At one positive in $34$ windows, F1 changes
noticeably when only a few samples cross the threshold. The AUC drop from
about $0.87$ to about $0.52$ in both models is less sensitive to this effect.
Six of the $40$ tuned thresholds also lie at the lower edge of the validation
sweep ($0.01$), two of them in the seed-$42$ gap decomposition. A finer sweep
could slightly raise the corresponding tuned F1 values.

\textbf{Early overfitting.} In $19$ of the $20$ runs, the validation AUC peaks
within the first eight epochs and then declines while the training loss keeps
falling. The reported checkpoint is the one at the peak. The horizon read-out
of the onset configurations (\cref{sec:method}) narrows in a similar way
during training, with the gap between its 5th and 95th validation percentiles
shrinking to near zero by the last epoch, compared with $0.13$ to $0.57$ at
the selected checkpoints of the pure, hedged and censored configurations. The
selection step is included in the spread across seeds.

\textbf{Order of decisions.} The detection metric was chosen after we had seen
the window-level results. Although it follows from the deployment requirement
and from quickest change detection~\cite{page1954cusum}, and was fixed in
\cref{sec:evalprotocol} before the seed replicates were run, readers should
keep this order in mind.

\section{Onset Timing Under Right-Censoring}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problems with the Binary Label}
By construction, the binary label of \cref{eq:binlabel} is wrong near the
cutoff and at the end of the footage, two situations typical of right-censored
time-to-event data forced into binary classification. Near the cutoff,
windows in which the crossing begins $H + 5$ frames after the last frame are
labelled negative and grouped with never-crossers, despite looking almost
identical to windows at $H - 5$ frames, which are labelled positive. The task
is ill-posed in this region, as only the position of the cutoff separates the
two labels. At the end of the footage, some windows have fewer than $H$
observed future frames and no crossing in them. Either label could be
correct for such windows, and assigning a negative label relies on an interval
that was never observed. Discarding them, as in \cref{sec:omits}, loses their
observed frames as well as the one fact known about their future: no crossing
began before the end of the footage.

In addition to the binary label, each streaming window is annotated with three
quantities: the number of frames $o$ between its last observed frame and the
next crossing onset (set to $-1$ if the remaining footage contains no onset),
the number $f$ of future frames available after the window, and a flag $c$
indicating whether the track contains a crossing at any point. In
survival-analysis terms, $o$ is the event time and $f$ the censoring time. The
flag $c$ is needed for windows without an onset in the remaining footage, to
distinguish pedestrians who crossed earlier in the track from those whose
observation is censored.

\subsection{Discrete-Time Hazard}
We divide the next $L$ frames into $K = L/w$ bins of width $w$ and write $h_k$
for the hazard of bin $k$, i.e., the probability that a first crossing begins
in that bin given that none has begun earlier. From the pooled representation,
the model outputs $K$ logits. The probability of an onset within a horizon
of $K_H = H/w$ bins then follows from the survival product
\begin{equation}
  P(\text{onset} \le H) = 1 - \prod_{k=0}^{K_H - 1}\left(1 - h_k\right).
  \label{eq:readout}
\end{equation}
The timing model reports the quantity given by the binary baselines and is
evaluated with their metric, since \cref{eq:readout} answers the question
posed by \cref{eq:binlabel}.

\subsection{Supervision Under Partial Observation}
The target $y_k$ and the mask $m_k$ of each bin are derived from this
annotation as illustrated in \cref{fig:cases}.
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{Onset within range} ($0 \le o < L$). With $e = \lfloor o/w
  \rfloor$, bins $0$ to $e-1$ are supervised as $0$ and bin $e$ as $1$. Bins
  after $e$ are masked, as the first crossing has already begun.
  \item \textbf{Onset beyond range} ($o \ge L$). All $K$ bins are supervised
  as $0$, since the observed crossing falls outside the look-ahead.
  \item \textbf{Censored} ($o < 0$, with some future observed). Bins covered
  by $f$ are supervised as $0$ and the rest are masked.
  \item \textbf{Already crossed} ($o < 0$ and $c$). Windows after the
  pedestrian's first crossing are removed from training.
\end{enumerate}
In binary labelling, case~3 cannot be expressed and case~4 is mislabelled as
negative.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\columnwidth]{fig4_hazard_cases.pdf}
  \caption{Supervision cases for the hazard target, showing bins contributing
  to the loss (shaded), masked bins receiving zero gradient (dashed) and the
  removal of the case~4 window from the risk set.}
  \label{fig:cases}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Training Objective}
We train with a masked binary cross-entropy over the bins:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{onset}} = -\sum_{k=0}^{K-1} m_k
    \Big[\, y_k \log h_k + (1 - y_k)\log(1 - h_k) \,\Big].
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
The mask sets the gradient of every bin after the onset or beyond the end of
the footage to exactly zero, which is verified in a unit test using automatic
differentiation. In addition, the implementation enforces two conditions on
the horizons. The look-ahead must be strictly longer than the reported horizon
($L > H$), as otherwise a crossing at $H + 5$ would again receive a flat
negative label. The read-out horizon must also match that of the binary
baselines, or \cref{eq:readout} would silently answer a different question
under the same metric name. This is checked by reproducing the binary label of
the generator from the timing read-out over a full track.

\subsection{Choosing the Look-Ahead and Bin Width}
The settings $L$ and $w$ affect only the training: the reported horizon
remains $H = 32$ frames (\cref{sec:horizon}). We chose $L$ on
the basis of the composition measured in \cref{sec:omits}. Since the
confusable band ends $64$ frames after $H$, a look-ahead of $L = 96$ frames
($3.2$\,s) covers the whole band, compared with $47\%$ of it at $L = 60$. The
number of training windows with a positive ``crossing begins here'' signal
also increases, from $2{,}530$ at $L = H$ to $6{,}716$. A wider look-ahead
would bring small gains, as two thirds of the hard temporal negatives lie more
than five seconds ahead. We use a bin width of $w = 4$, which gives $K = 24$
bins, $8$ of them inside the reported horizon. Positives are then about four
times denser per bin, at a cost of $133$\,ms of timing resolution.

\subsection{Configurations of the Objective}
Three configurations are obtained by changing only the loss weights. In the
\emph{auxiliary} configuration, we report the output of the binary head and use
the hazard term as a regulariser. The \emph{pure} configuration
uses the hazard as the only training objective, with the reported output given
by \cref{eq:readout}. In this configuration the optimised quantity differs
from the reported one. A term on the read-out is therefore added in the
\emph{hedged} configuration. A fully observed window contributes approximately $17$ nats
at initialisation, compared with $5.5$ for a window with only the $32$ future
frames guaranteed during generation, since \cref{eq:loss} is a sum over the
observed bins. We scale the hazard term down when it is used with the binary
objective.

\textbf{Collapse of the hazard head.} Each of the $K$ bins sees a positive in
approximately $2.9\%/K$ of the windows. The head can then lower the loss by
predicting $h \approx 0$ everywhere. We detect this failure as a narrow peak
of the read-out at the base rate across validation windows. The bin width
$w = 4$ was chosen to mitigate it.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:evalprotocol}
A detector on a vehicle is judged on detection rate, lead time and
false-alarm rate. We evaluate the three together. Each window of the streaming
test split yields one decision, giving \decisionsPerHour{} decisions per hour
at stride $3$ and $30$\,fps. A crossing pedestrian counts as detected if any
window whose crossing still lies ahead scores above the threshold. The lead
time is the largest such offset, i.e., the earliest warning the model would
have given. Alarms raised after the person has stepped out are ignored. The
test split has \Ncrossers{} crossing and \Nnoncrossers{} never-crossing
pedestrians. Every window of a never-crossing pedestrian is an opportunity for
a false alarm. We sweep the threshold to trace detection rate against alarm
rate and read it at budgets of $1200$, $460$, $205$, $95$ and $41$ alarms per
hour. Each budget fixes the lowest threshold whose realised alarm rate
stays within it. This accounting is taken from quickest change
detection~\cite{page1954cusum}, in which detection delay has been measured
against the time between false alarms since the 1950s. We do not use its
cumulative-sum (CUSUM) detector. CUSUM assumes a persistent shift, whereas
the approach to a crossing is a brief change.

\textbf{Accounting rules.} \texttt{per\_window} accounting counts every
alarming window. Under \texttt{per\_track}
accounting, a never-crossing pedestrian who triggers any alarm counts as a
single false alarm, as a driver would experience it. Both rules divide by the
same exposure time. We name the rule for every number, as the two rules
disagree at loose budgets.

\textbf{Pre-registration.} The budgets, accounting rules, metrics and success
criterion were fixed before we ran the seed replicates. The criterion is met
if the mean detection rate of the pure hazard configuration exceeds that of
the binary baseline by more than the sum of their standard deviations at three
or more of the five budgets. Any smaller difference is reported as
inconclusive. AUC and tuned F1 are reported in addition (\cref{sec:windowtie}).

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}
\textbf{Binary baseline.} As a baseline, we train the model of \cref{sec:gap}
with a single crossing head on the streaming protocol, using the standard
cross-entropy objective at $H = 32$. Apart from the loss, the onset
configurations are trained in the same way and with the same seeds.

\textbf{Onset configurations.} In addition to the auxiliary, pure and hedged
weightings of \cref{sec:method}, a fourth configuration (pure $+$ censored) is
trained including the \seedconf{7\,470} right-censored windows otherwise
discarded by the generator. The auxiliary configuration is scored by its
binary head. The other three are scored by $P(\text{onset} \le 32)$ from
\cref{eq:readout}, the quantity the baseline also reports.

\textbf{Published methods.} Published crossing-intention methods were designed
for the anchored protocol, and retraining them would mainly measure the
quality of our reimplementation. We do not include them, as the binary
baseline isolates the one variable changed in this paper: the training
objective.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{How Far Ahead Crossing Can Be Predicted}
Some crossings in the confusable band may not be predictable at all. A
pedestrian may walk along the pavement and then turn suddenly into the road.
Three seconds earlier, the decision may not have been made yet, or may not yet
be visible in the body. No choice of input, architecture or loss would allow a
model to predict such a crossing, and the proportion of such cases in the band
is unknown. A realistic goal is a model that is confident where evidence
exists and uncertain where it is missing. This is a question of ranking and
calibration, corresponding to the failure measured in our experiments.

We located this limit by comparing the scores of windows at a given time to
onset with those of never-crossing pedestrians. For the binary and pure hazard
objectives, AUC is $0.81$ to $0.82$ within the first second, $0.72$ to $0.73$
between two and three seconds, $0.64$ to $0.66$ from five to ten seconds, and
$0.52$ to $0.54$, i.e., chance level, beyond ten seconds. This decrease is
much larger than the seed spread, which remains below $0.03$ in each range,
and the curves of the two objectives agree to within $0.02$. The
anchored-trained model shows the reverse profile (\cref{sec:whowhen}).
From this input, crossing is therefore predictable for roughly ten seconds at
most, suggesting a limit for any method using the same input on PIE and
supporting the short horizon chosen in \cref{sec:horizon}. We have not found
this measurement in the crossing-intention literature, although it requires only one evaluation pass over a trained
model.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:ablations}
Besides the issues in \cref{sec:threats}, all results come from PIE, recorded in one city in daylight and clear weather. Replication on JAAD would show whether the effect is specific to this dataset. The
streaming-trained entries also rest on checkpoints selected at an early
validation peak, which may not show the best these models can do. We have not
yet run several planned ablations: varying the look-ahead over $L \in \{60, 96, 150\}$ and the
bin width over $w \in \{4, 8\}$ to test the sizing argument of
\cref{sec:method}, focal and class-balanced losses on the binary objective to
separate our claim from a claim about class imbalance, and a comparison of
frame-pooling rules.

\subsection{Implications for Evaluation Practice}
If $\Ghard \approx \Gtot$ holds more widely, a model's anchored score says
little about its behaviour on a stream, and the standard protocol has
left the safety-critical case out of training and testing. This is cheap to
check, as the streaming sampler only relabels existing annotations. Recent work revisiting the definitions and
metrics used on PIE and JAAD~\cite{rasouli2024diving} shows that the standard
evaluation is already questioned, and our results suggest that the
sampling protocol deserves part of that attention. At $205$ alarms per hour,
three seeds of our binary objective also detect between $38.5\%$ and
$44.4\%$ of the crossing pedestrians, a range as wide as the largest gap we
measured between objectives. We therefore recommend reporting the run-to-run
spread in every streaming evaluation on PIE and treating a difference between
methods as evidence only when it exceeds this spread.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The event-anchored protocol and the streaming condition contain different
negatives and pose different tasks. We measured what the anchored protocol leaves out: a quarter of the
streaming training windows and two fifths of the test windows show pedestrians
who cross later. A model trained under the anchored protocol falls close to
chance ranking on the streaming test set, and threshold re-tuning recovers
almost none of the loss. Because part of the difficulty lies in the
labelling, we proposed onset timing under right-censoring. A window whose future footage runs out then counts as censored and a pedestrian who has already crossed leaves the risk set. The reported horizon probability remains directly comparable with the binary baselines.

Whether the reformulation detects crossings better remains open. Measured against a criterion fixed before the runs, with three seeds of each objective and detection rate at matched false-alarm budgets, it is indistinguishable from binary training. The practical finding concerns the run-to-run spread, which is
as wide as the largest gap between objectives: at a $33.9{:}1$ base rate, a claim about an objective has to be supported by several runs, whether it rests on window-level scores or on detection curves.
