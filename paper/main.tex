\begin{abstract}
Pedestrian crossing prediction for autonomous vehicles is usually benchmarked
with an event-anchored protocol, which labels a few windows per pedestrian, one
to two seconds before a reference moment, by whether the pedestrian crosses.
However, the vehicle observes each pedestrian continuously and must decide at
every moment whether they are about to cross, a setting we call streaming. We
show that the streaming question has two parts: whether a pedestrian will cross
in front of the vehicle at all (\emph{who}) and, if so, when the crossing starts
(\emph{when}). The anchored protocol ignores the latter. On the Pedestrian
Intention Estimation dataset, anchored-trained neural networks and boosted
trees rank who crosses well, but their ranking of when crossings start is at or
below chance. Their area under the receiver operating characteristic curve
drops from \V{abs.deep.anc} and \V{abs.tree.anc} on anchored data to
\V{abs.deep.str} and \V{abs.tree.str} on streaming data. Adding scene
information raises the \emph{who} score from \V{scene.base.who} to
\V{scene.RTS.who}, but no input we added raises the \emph{when} score by more
than \V{audit.when.maxgain.abs}. We also tested a hazard objective that
predicts when the crossing starts. With the same training windows, it does no
better than binary training, consistent with inputs that carry little timing
information.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A pedestrian-crossing detector on a moving vehicle must make a prediction at
every moment for every pedestrian in view. Most of those moments do not
precede a crossing. The standard evaluation protocol asks a narrower question.
Introduced with the Pedestrian Intention Estimation (PIE) and Joint Attention
in Autonomous Driving (JAAD) datasets~\cite{rasouli2019pie,rasouli2017jaad},
and later standardised in an evaluation suite~\cite{kotseruba2021benchmark},
it samples a few windows from each pedestrian track. These windows end one to
two seconds before a reference moment and are labelled according to whether
the pedestrian crosses (\cref{fig:protocols}a).

Interpreting a score from this protocol requires separating two questions. The
first is \emph{who}: will the pedestrian cross in front of the vehicle at all?
The second is \emph{when}: if the pedestrian will cross, will the crossing
begin within the next second? A streaming detector has to answer both. It
should remain silent, for example, when a pedestrian will cross only five
seconds later (\cref{fig:protocols}b). We show that the streaming label is the
product of these two components. The anchored protocol holds the second
component fixed and therefore scores only the first. A single anchored score
cannot reveal which component makes the streaming task difficult.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{fig1_protocols.pdf}
  \caption{The two sampling protocols on one pedestrian track. (a)~Anchored
  windows end a known interval before the crossing and carry the label $C$
  (does the pedestrian cross), which scores \emph{who} only. (b)~Streaming
  windows cover the whole track, and their label combines \emph{who} and
  \emph{when}. Windows are drawn further apart than in practice, where they
  are \V{c.T} frames long and start every \V{c.stride} frames.}
  \label{fig:protocols}
\end{figure*}

We trained a neural network and boosted trees on the same pose and kinematic
inputs. We then added scene, behaviour and oracle inputs to the trees, alone
and combined, and the scene inputs also to the network; the oracles provide
information unavailable to a deployed system. Each study was specified in
writing and time-stamped before its first result. The study makes five
contributions:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{Decomposition.} We factor the streaming crossing label into
  \emph{who} and \emph{when}, corresponding to incidence and latency in a
  mixture cure model from survival analysis. The anchored label estimates
  \emph{who} alone (\cref{sec:formulation}).
  \item \textbf{An explanation of the protocol gap.} In both model families,
  anchored training ranks \emph{who} well but reverses the ordering of
  \emph{when}. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve
  (AUC) falls to chance on streaming data. Neither threshold retuning nor
  re-weighted losses remove this gap, and the smaller size of the anchored
  training set does not explain it (\cref{sec:gap}).
  \item \textbf{Decomposed evaluation.} We score \emph{who} and \emph{when}
  separately and count false alarms both per window and per pedestrian. These
  two ways of counting lead to different conclusions (\cref{sec:audit}).
  \item \textbf{An information audit.} Scene information raises \emph{who}
  above a human intention rating, although that comparison is post hoc.
  \emph{When} improves by at most \V{audit.when.maxgain.abs}, less than half
  the gain in \emph{who} (\cref{sec:audit}).
  \item \textbf{A timing objective.} On the same training windows, an
  objective that models time to crossing does no better than binary training
  in either model family. This agrees with the decomposition if the inputs
  contain no timing information beyond what binary training already uses
  (\cref{sec:objective}).
\end{enumerate}
Streaming evaluation is established in online action
detection~\cite{degeest2016oad,shou2018odas}, and traffic research has
distinguished immediate crossing from waiting time (\cref{sec:related}).
However, to our knowledge and based on a targeted search, no previous study of
crossing prediction from vehicle video separates \emph{who} from \emph{when} or
shows that the anchored score measures only \emph{who}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Crossing Prediction on PIE and JAAD}
Most studies using PIE~\cite{rasouli2019pie} and JAAD~\cite{rasouli2017jaad}
follow the benchmark of Kotseruba \emph{et al.}~\cite{kotseruba2021benchmark}.
They assign labels per track and use an observation period ending one to two
seconds before the crossing. Several studies have questioned what the
resulting scores measure. Bounding boxes alone can yield high accuracy on
PIE~\cite{achaji2022bbox}, and models depend heavily on ego-vehicle
speed, which may reflect the driver's behaviour rather than the
pedestrian's~\cite{azarmi2024feature}.

Rasouli and Kotseruba~\cite{rasouli2024diving} point out that intention and
action are often used interchangeably. They also report much higher
action-prediction scores when the ego vehicle is stationary, a setting in
which most samples are crossers. Kotseruba \emph{et al.}~\cite{kotseruba2020want}
provide a human reference for assessing a pedestrian's intention to cross. Yao
\emph{et al.}~\cite{yao2021coupling} report results for both whole tracks and
an ``event of crossing'' setting restricted to the one to two seconds before
crossing. Most of these studies retain the window population of the anchored
protocol, and none separates the label into \emph{who} and \emph{when}. Our
evaluation changes both the sampled windows and the label.

\subsection{When Pedestrians Start to Cross}
Early work on path prediction found that a pedestrian approaching the kerb
could be reliably classified as stopping or crossing only a fraction of a
second before the event~\cite{keller2014cross}. Later work incorporated
context, including awareness of the vehicle and distance to the kerb, to
predict transitions between walking and standing~\cite{kooij2014context}. More
recent studies predict whether a pedestrian will start or stop walking within
two seconds~\cite{guo2022stopgo}, collect frame-by-frame intent labels from
many annotators~\cite{jing2025psi}, and report crossing-prediction performance
against time to the event~\cite{gtranspdm2024}. These studies model timing or
vary it in evaluation. We show that the standard score is insensitive to it.

\subsection{Online Action Detection and Time-to-Event Modelling}
Online action detection assigns a label to each frame in a stream using only
present and past evidence~\cite{degeest2016oad}. In the action-start variant,
the system must signal the beginning of an action as early as
possible~\cite{shou2018odas}. We adopt this streaming setting and its approach
to counting false alarms.

Survival analysis models time to an event when some observations end before
the event occurs~\cite{cox1972regression}. Mixture cure models separate
whether an event occurs from when it occurs~\cite{farewell1982cure,kuk1992mixture},
giving the structure used in \cref{sec:factor}. In traffic research, survival
and duration models have been used to describe pedestrians' waiting times at
the kerb~\cite{hamed2001waiting,li2013waiting}. Yang
\emph{et al.}~\cite{aft2015waiting} combine a logit model for pedestrians who
cross immediately with a duration model for those who wait, a separation close
to ours. We use the cure structure
to interpret an existing vision benchmark and use a hazard objective for time
to crossing as a further check (\cref{sec:objective}).

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

\subsection{Event-Anchored Sampling}
For a pedestrian tracked with annotated frames $1, \dots, n$, our
implementation of the anchored protocol~\cite{kotseruba2021benchmark} takes up
to six windows of $T$ frames, whose last frames lie \V{c.anc.tte.min} to
\V{c.anc.tte.max} frames (one to two seconds) before a reference frame
annotated by PIE. For a pedestrian who crosses, this reference frame is the
start of the crossing; for a pedestrian who does not cross, this is the last
frame in which the pedestrian is visible~\cite{kotseruba2021benchmark}. All
windows extracted from a track receive the same track-level label (whether the
pedestrian crosses or not), therefore the class ratio reflects how many
crossing (positive) and non-crossing (negative) tracks were sampled. In PIE's
test set, the ratio is 1 positive sample for every 2.6 negative samples.

\subsection{Streaming Sampling}
In our streaming protocol, a pedestrian track is sampled beginning from the
first frame, and windows are extracted every few frames along the whole track.
The window ending at frame $t$ is labelled:
\begin{equation}
  y_t = \mathbf{1}\left[\, \exists\ \text{onset in } (t,\, t+H] \,\right],
  \label{eq:binlabel}
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the anticipation horizon. Windows in which the pedestrian is
already crossing are discarded. The positive class ratio is in this case the
ratio of windows in which a crossing is about to begin. In the PIE dataset,
this accounts for \V{data.train.pos}\% of the training windows, or one
positive sample for every 33.9 negative samples. Despite the seemingly obvious
significant imbalance, the two ratios measure different quantities, rather
than one task being outright more difficult than the other.

\subsection{Three Kinds of Streaming Negative}
\label{sec:taxonomy}
The streaming protocol produces three types of negative sample
(\cref{fig:protocols}b):
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{Never-crosser windows}: pedestrians who wait or walk alongside
  the pavement and do not cross on camera.
  \item \textbf{Hard temporal negatives}: pedestrians whose crossing starts at
  more than $H$ after the window ends. These windows are labelled negative;
  however, they show the same person with near identical features with the
  positive windows that follow, and only the position of the cutoff gives them
  a different label.
  \item \textbf{Already-crossed}: pedestrians who have finished crossing, which
  are labelled 0 like never-crossers.
\end{itemize}
Only the first kind of negative sample is produced by the anchored protocol.

\subsection{The Streaming Label Factors into Who and When}
\label{sec:factor}
% Claim: the streaming label is the product of a "who" and a "when" probability.
The streaming label of \cref{eq:binlabel} combines two questions: whether the
pedestrian will cross at all, and whether the crossing starts within the
horizon. For a window ending at frame $t$, let $x_t$ denote what the model
observes (the last $T$ frames). Let $\tau$ be the frame at which the
pedestrian's next crossing in front of the vehicle begins ($\tau = \infty$ if
none follows before the track ends). We write $C = \mathbf{1}[\tau < \infty]$
for the indicator that the pedestrian crosses at all. A window is \emph{at
risk} unless it lies after the pedestrian's last crossing. On at-risk windows,
$y_t = \mathbf{1}[\tau \le t + H]$, and a window can only be positive if the
pedestrian crosses ($C = 1$), so the chain rule gives
\begin{equation}
  P(y_t{=}1 \mid x_t)
  = \underbrace{P(C{=}1 \mid x_t)}_{\textit{who}}
    \,
    \underbrace{P(\tau \le t{+}H \mid C{=}1, x_t)}_{\textit{when}}.
  \label{eq:factor}
\end{equation}
The \emph{who} factor is the probability that the pedestrian crosses at all,
which differs from intent: a pedestrian who intends to cross may not cross
while the vehicle is present (\cref{sec:who}). The \emph{when} factor is the
probability that a crosser starts within the horizon. Already-crossed windows
keep the label $0$; they count as negatives in window-level metrics but are
excluded from the scores below and from the detection curve
(\cref{sec:setup}).

% Link: the mixture cure model, why it fits, and what it gives this paper.
\Cref{eq:factor} has the structure of the mixture cure model of survival
analysis~\cite{farewell1982cure,kuk1992mixture}, which separates whether an
event occurs (its \emph{incidence}, here \emph{who}) from when it occurs (its
\emph{latency}, here \emph{when}). Unlike standard survival models, it allows
subjects who never have the event, called \emph{cured}. This fits our setting,
as most pedestrians in PIE never cross in front of the vehicle
(\cref{sec:setup}). We use the
model only to interpret the label and do not fit it to our data. First,
survival models can learn from subjects whose observation ends before the
event. In our data, such subjects are the windows whose future was not
recorded (censored windows), which a binary label must drop or set to
$0$ (\cref{sec:objective}). Second, a subject whose observation ends before
any event cannot be distinguished from a cured one. A track that ends without
a crossing cannot show whether the pedestrian would have crossed later, so $C$
is defined on the observed track only (\cref{sec:perwindow}).

% Claim: the anchored protocol estimates "who" only.
The anchored protocol estimates only \emph{who}. Its label is $C$, which records whether the pedestrian crosses but
not when the crossing starts. Its sampling also holds \emph{when} nearly
fixed, as every window ends \V{c.anc.tte.min} to \V{c.anc.tte.max} frames
before the reference frame. A model can therefore reach a high anchored score
even if it orders imminent and distant onsets arbitrarily.

% Definition: the two factor scores.
Both factors are scored with an AUC of the model's crossing score $s_t$, so no
extra output is needed. For \emph{who}, each pedestrian is represented by the
mean $s_t$ over their at-risk windows, and crossers are ranked against
never-crossers. For \emph{when}, only the windows of crossers are used: those
whose crossing starts within $H$ are ranked against those whose crossing
starts later (hard temporal negatives). A value below 0.5 means that timing
is ranked backwards.
Because this score pools the windows of all crossers, it can also be shifted
by how high a model scores each crosser overall, so we also report it
averaged within each crosser (\cref{sec:whowhen}).

% Consequences, checked later; disclosed as written after the measurements.
The decomposition implies three consequences, which we label P1 to P3 and
compare with the results of later sections:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item[\textbf{P1}] A threshold does not change the order of
  the windows, so retuning it cannot correct a reversed \emph{when} score and
  should recover almost none of the anchored-to-streaming gap (\cref{sec:matrix}).
  \item[\textbf{P2}] On the same training windows, an objective that models the time to the
  crossing can beat binary training only if the inputs carry timing information
  that binary training leaves unused (\cref{sec:objective}).
  \item[\textbf{P3}] Information
  that improves only \emph{who} should raise detection more when false alarms
  are counted per pedestrian than per window (\cref{sec:setup}). When alarms are
  counted per window, a few never-crossers who wait near the road for a long
  time can still use up the whole budget (\cref{sec:perwindow}).
\end{itemize}
All three results were measured before the decomposition was written down;
they agree with it but were not pre-registered tests.

\section{Composition of the Streaming Windows}

05 for validation and 03 for testing. Streaming windows have $T = \V{c.T}$

\section{The Protocol Gap, Explained}
\label{sec:gap}

\subsection{Experiment Set Up}
\label{sec:setup}
We use two learners for the evaluation of this task: a temporal
neural-network and a gradient-boosted tree. Both learners see the same input:
a \V{c.T}-frame window per sample, with each frame being a 58-dimensional
vector of 49 pose measurements from 23 whole-body keypoints estimated with
DWPose~\cite{yang2023dwpose} and 9 channels for bounding box position, size,
velocity, and ego-vehicle speed. The neural network standardises each channel,
encodes the window with two temporal convolutions, a two-layer gated recurrent
unit and multi-head self-attention of width 128 (0.66\,M parameters), and
pools per-frame crossing scores with log-sum-exp. It was trained with a
sampler that oversamples crossing windows, a learning rate of $10^{-4}$ and at
most 30 epochs, and selected on validation AUC, with \V{c.deep.seeds} seeds.
The boosted trees summarise each channel by its last value, mean, change, and
standard deviation over the window (232 features). They were fitted by
gradient boosting~\cite{friedman2001greedy} in scikit-learn with 300
iterations and 31 leaves, cut at the best validation iteration, with
\V{c.tree.seeds} seeds. On this input the trees detect more crossers than the
neural network and vary less between seeds, which makes comparisons between
tree models more precise, although we do not claim that trees are better
models in general.

\textbf{Detection curve.} A driver needs one timely warning for each crossing
pedestrian at a tolerable number of false alarms, which window-level scores
such as AUC and F1 do not measure. We therefore evaluate every model with a
detection curve. Each test window is one decision (whether to raise an alarm),
so one hour of one pedestrian's footage gives 36\,000 decisions. A crossing
pedestrian counts as detected if any of their windows before the onset scores
above the threshold, and the earliest such window gives the lead time (how
long before the onset the first warning came). Alarms raised after the onset
are ignored.

Instead of fixing the threshold, we fix a false-alarm budget (false alarms per
hour of footage) of 1200, 460, 205, 95 or 41. Each budget sets the lowest threshold whose
alarm rate stays within it, so a model cannot gain detections by raising more
alarms.

False alarms are counted per window or per pedestrian, and every number names
its count. Per window, 205 alarms per hour lets 0.57\% of never-crosser
windows raise an alarm. Per pedestrian, a never-crosser who raises any alarm
is counted once, so a pedestrian who waits at the kerb for a long time costs
one alarm instead of many. The test split holds \V{peds.cross} crossing and
\V{peds.never} never-crossing pedestrians, with 37\,316 never-crosser windows
(1.04 hours of footage), so at 205 alarms per hour up to
\V{budget.205.alarms} never-crossers (43\%) may raise an alarm. At the
loosest budget, every model detects all crossers when counted per pedestrian,
therefore the per-pedestrian verdicts in \cref{sec:audit} use the other four
budgets.

\textbf{Decision rule.} With only \V{peds.cross} crossing pedestrians in the
test split, a detection rate depends on which pedestrians happen to be in it.
Before each study, we therefore fixed one rule for deciding whether a gap
between two models is larger than chance, and every detection comparison below
follows it unless marked post hoc.

The rule uses a paired bootstrap~\cite{efron1993bootstrap}. We draw
pedestrians from the test split at random with replacement, score both models
on the same draw, and record the gap between their detection rates. Seeds are
drawn in the same way. Pedestrians are drawn rather than windows, because the
windows of one pedestrian overlap and are not independent. After 2\,000 draws,
the middle 95\% of the gaps forms the 95\% interval. If the interval excludes
zero, one model was ahead in almost every draw.

A model is called better if its interval excludes zero at \V{c.verdict.k} or
more of the five budgets (a majority, so that no single budget decides the
verdict). The two models are
called equivalent if the interval lies within $\pm\V{c.margin}$ points at
\V{c.verdict.k} or more budgets~\cite{lakens2017equivalence}, about the
smallest gap our test split can resolve (\cref{sec:limitations}). Otherwise
the result is inconclusive: the gap did not clear the bar, which does not mean
the models are equal. The per-window count is the primary one. For the neural
networks, whose runs raise barely correlated alarms, we use a simpler rule: a
gap between seed means larger than the sum of their standard deviations, at
\V{c.verdict.k} or more budgets.

\subsection{The Cross-Protocol Matrix}
\label{sec:matrix}
In \cref{tab:matrix}, each model was trained under one protocol and tested
under both anchored and streaming test sets. The neural model and tree trained on
the anchored protocol both scored well on anchored test data
(\V{deep.R2anc.anc.auc} and \V{tree.A.anc.auc} AUC)
and fell to blind-guessing or inverse signal on streaming test data
(\V{deep.R2anc.str.auc} and \V{tree.A.auc} AUC).
Retuning the decision threshold does not rescue the F1 score, as expected from
P1 (\cref{sec:factor}) and from the low AUC score. Focal
loss~\cite{lin2017focal} and class-balanced
weights~\cite{cui2019classbalanced} were also tested for the anchored trained
neural network, which scored an AUC of 0.545 and 0.539, below the
pre-registered bar of 0.60, and the gain from retuning remained at $-$0.006
and +0.001.

\subsection{Who and When}
\label{sec:whowhen}
In \cref{tab:whowhen}, the same model and tree are scored on the two factors
of \cref{sec:factor}. Both of them when trained under the anchored protocol
ranked the \emph{who} question well at \V{deep.R2anc.who} and \V{tree.A.who}
AUC, while the \emph{when} question received inverse signal answer at
\V{deep.R2anc.when} and \V{tree.A.when} AUC. This means that among all
crossing pedestrians' windows, those far from onset are scored higher by both
models than those nearer to it. When averaged within each crossing pedestrian,
\emph{when} moved closer to blind-guessing at 0.477 and 0.438 AUC (post hoc).
Models trained under streaming protocol scored lower on \emph{who}
(\cref{tab:whowhen}) but significantly higher on \emph{when}. This is partly
because \emph{who} averages a crosser's scores over all their windows, and a
model that has learned timing scores windows further from the onset lower.
Separation from never-crossers also rose with time-to-onset for anchored
trained models and vice versa for streaming trained models
(\cref{fig:separability}). This pattern is expected from the anchored
sampling, where positive windows are fixed \V{c.anc.tte.min} to
\V{c.anc.tte.max} frames before the crossing and the last moments before a
crossing never appear in anchored training.

\section{Where the Difficulty Lies}
\label{sec:audit}

\subsection{The Information Audit}
To analyse the influence of input features on the \emph{who} and \emph{when}
factors, we add input groups to the pedestrian-only streaming protocol tree,
each on its own and combined (\cref{tab:audit}), and keep every other setting
fixed. The three groups are \emph{lights} (state of the largest visible
vehicle traffic light and pedestrian signal), \emph{traffic} (number of
vehicles and distance to the nearest), and \emph{layout} (intersection type,
lanes, signals, crosswalk distance). We also test two non-deployable oracles:
PIE's human intention rating (give the pedestrian scene to a real human and
ask whether the pedestrian will cross), and the ego-vehicle's speed change
over the next second, which is confounded, as drivers slow down near crossing
pedestrians.

\subsection{The Question of Who Improves with Scene Information}
\label{sec:who}
With scene information, \emph{who} rose from \V{scene.base.who} to
\V{scene.RTS.who} (\cref{tab:audit}). This is above the human intention
rating, which separated crossers from never-crossers at \V{ph.human.who} (a
post hoc comparison). Most of the gain came from layout, while lights and traffic alone added less.
That a model can rank \emph{who} better than the rating is expected, since the
rating estimates intent (\cref{sec:factor}): 373 of the
\V{peds.never} never-crossers (75\%) were rated at least
0.5 (a post hoc count). Counted per pedestrian, detection
rose by +6.3, +10.7
and +10.3 points at 205, 95 and 41 alarms per hour
(pre-registered secondary count: better). Counted per window, the primary
count, the gain of +1.3 to
+6.2 points was inconclusive.

\subsection{The Question of When Improves Little}
\label{sec:when}
The gain in \emph{when} was much smaller. With scene information, it rose by
\V{audit.when.gain.abs}, less than half the increase in \emph{who}
(\V{audit.who.gain.abs}), and no input group or combination raised it by more
than \V{audit.when.maxgain.abs}. In a second pre-registered study, the neural
network received the scene groups as extra input at every frame; its
\emph{who} rose from \V{dscene.base.who} to \V{dscene.scene.who} and its
\emph{when} from \V{dscene.base.when} to \V{dscene.scene.when}, again less
than half as much
(\cref{tab:audit}).

In the pre-registered test for \emph{when}, we added the pedestrian's waiting
history at the kerb, which is regularly modelled in traffic
studies~\cite{hamed2001waiting,li2013waiting,aft2015waiting}. The resulting
gain of +0.006 counted as no gain under the pre-registered rule, which
requires a gain of at least 0.02 with a 95\% interval that excludes zero.
Similarly, looking history added +0.003, and both histories together also
added only +0.003. The human intent oracle carries no timing information: it
raised the \emph{who} score while \emph{when} fell slightly to
\V{scene.H.when}, and the ego-vehicle's speed oracle barely raised it by
+0.004.

Separation from never-crosser and the \emph{when} score both depend heavily
on how far ahead the crossing is. Within a second of onset, all streaming
trained models across various input group additions ranked positive windows
above never-crosser windows at 0.809 to 0.918 AUC, whereas beyond 10 seconds
the ranking fell to blind-guessing again between 0.470 and 0.584 AUC
(\cref{fig:separability}). Scene information and the oracles lifted the curve
while preserving the shape.

Far from the onset, the model scored a hard temporal negative window like a
never-crosser window, therefore these windows are easily ranked below positive
windows. Against the \V{ph.wd.share.bge10}\% of hard temporal negatives whose
crossing starts ten seconds or more after the window, every streaming-trained
model exceeded 0.80 (post hoc), which keeps the pooled
\emph{when} score high. Against those whose crossing starts within two
seconds, the models stayed between \V{ph.wd.near.min} and
\V{ph.wd.near.max}; scene information added 0.011 to 0.050 per distance range.

\subsection{Why Per-Window Detection Lags}
\label{sec:perwindow}
A better \emph{who} score improved detection per pedestrian but not clearly per
window, as expected from P3 (\cref{sec:factor}). Scene information followed
this pattern in both learners: detection was better per pedestrian and
inconclusive per window (both verdicts pre-registered). For the neural
network, detection per window even fell at every budget, from
\V{dscene.base.det.pw.205} to \V{dscene.scene.det.pw.205}\% at 205 alarms per
hour.

The analysis of this difference below is post hoc. With scene information,
the \V{c.fa.windows205} false-alarm windows of the trees allowed at 205 per
hour came from fewer never-crossers (39.4 to 25.4, mean over seeds). However,
the three never-crossers with the most alarms held more of these windows (63.6
and then 78.2). For the neural network, the alarms came from 35 and then 15
never-crossers. For one tree with scene information, 22 of the 24
never-crossers raising these alarms stood at signalised locations (slightly
more than the 82.2\% of all test windows), with a median intention rating of
0.90. They were mostly intending pedestrians waiting to cross, often while the
ego vehicle was itself waiting at a red light. Under our labels they are
\emph{who} errors, as they did not cross while the vehicle was present
($C = 0$), although their ratings suggest that many would have crossed after
the footage ends (\cref{sec:factor}).

\section{Does a Timing Objective Help?}
\label{sec:objective}

\subsection{Onset Timing Under Censoring}
\label{sec:method}
An objective that models the time to the crossing, which we call the hazard
objective, could use timing information that the binary label leaves unused.
Starting from the ending of the observation window, the next $L = 96$ frames
were divided into
$K = 24$ bins of $w = 4$ frames, and a hazard $h_k$ was
predicted for each bin: the probability that the next crossing starts in bin
$k$ if it has not started before. The probability of an onset within $H$ then
follows as
\begin{equation}
  P(\text{onset} \le H) = 1 - \prod_{k=0}^{H/w - 1}\left(1 - h_k\right),
  \label{eq:readout}
\end{equation}
which answers the question of \cref{eq:binlabel}, so a hazard model can be
scored exactly like a binary one. Targets $z_k$ and masks $m_k$ were derived
from the frames to the next onset, the frames of future observed and whether
the track contains a crossing. Bins after an observed onset and bins beyond
the end of the footage were masked, and already-crossed windows were removed.
The loss was the masked binary cross-entropy
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = -\sum_{k=0}^{K-1} m_k
    \Big[\, z_k \log h_k + (1 - z_k)\log(1 - h_k) \,\Big].
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
Masked bins receive zero gradient, which allowed the 7\,470 censored
training windows to be used, whereas binary training has to drop them or label
them 0. In the neural network, a hazard
output replaced the binary one. For the trees, the hazard was fitted with the
exact discrete-time likelihood, using one training row per observed bin and
the bin index as an extra feature. At longer horizons, the bins were widened
with the horizon, and these arms used three seeds.

pre-registered bar. The result therefore favours binary training, but is
ultimately inconclusive.

\textbf{Censored windows helped the hazard objective.} When the 7\,470
censored windows were added, tree detection rate per window increased by +5.9
to +8.5 points (pre-registered). For binary training, labelling these windows
0 gave +2.1 to +5.2 points over leaving them out, although this post hoc
contrast was inconclusive. The zero labels raised \emph{who} AUC score from
\V{tree.B.who} to \V{tree.Bz32.who}, suggesting that dropping these windows in
our binary training loses some information.

\textbf{Longer horizons.} How censored windows are handled matters more at
longer horizons, because a longer horizon leaves more windows without a full
future. We therefore repeated the tree comparison at horizons of 2.13 and
5.33\,s. Against binary training that drops
censored windows, the lead of the hazard objective grew with the horizon, from
+2.3 to +3.5 points at \V{c.Hsec}\,s (inconclusive) to +10.1 to +16.4 points
at 5.33\,s (better), as predicted before the experiment. This is because
dropping is biased: when a never-crosser's footage ends, their last windows
are dropped, while a crosser's windows are kept whenever the crossing is seen. Against binary training that labels censored windows
0, however, the hazard objective was inconclusive at every horizon. The hazard
objective therefore beats dropping, not binary training as such.

\textbf{Reading.} These results agree with P2 (\cref{sec:factor}). The hazard
objective adds supervision about when a crossing starts, which can only help
if the inputs carry timing information that binary training leaves unused.
\Cref{sec:audit} found little such information in our inputs, and the
\emph{when} scores of the two objectives were close (\cref{tab:objective}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What This Means for Evaluation}
An anchored score measures \emph{who} alone. It can be high even when a model
reverses the ordering of crossing times (\cref{tab:whowhen}), so it tells us
little about how the model will behave on streaming data. Three changes to
evaluation could be made with the existing annotations. \emph{Who} and
\emph{when} should be reported separately. Evaluation should use streaming
windows and a detection curve at fixed false-alarm budgets. False alarms
should also be counted per pedestrian as well as per window, because a few
waiting pedestrians can dominate the per-window count.

\subsection{What When Would Need}
\emph{When} remains the open problem. The most persistent false alarms,
examined post hoc, were \emph{who} errors involving intending pedestrians at signalised
crosswalks who might cross after the footage ends. Their crossing time may
depend on information missing from the data, such as the signal controlling
the pedestrian, gaps in oncoming traffic or interaction with the driver.
Frame-level intent labels~\cite{jing2025psi}, longer footage after the
observation window and waiting-time
models~\cite{hamed2001waiting,li2013waiting,aft2015waiting} are natural next
steps.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\textbf{Pre-registration.} All studies were specified and time-stamped before
their first result, with two amendments. Before any results were obtained, we
loosened a check that the exported inputs reproduced the earlier network
outputs, because those stored outputs had been computed in half precision.
After the results were obtained, we found that the tree hazard readout had
been stored in single precision. This rounded confident scores to exactly one,
leaving three hazard arms with no threshold that could meet the tightest alarm
budgets. Recomputing the readout in double precision changed two
verdicts---from ``binary better'' to inconclusive---for the tree hazard
comparisons against binary training, on the full input and on the input
without ego-vehicle speed. Both versions are retained.

The pre-registered verdicts not discussed above were all inconclusive per
window: lights, traffic and layout individually against the pedestrian-only
model; the scene with both oracles against that model, although this was
better per pedestrian; the intent oracle beyond the scene; the detection
contrasts in the waiting audit; hazard against binary training on an eighth, a
quarter and half of the training pedestrians; focal loss and class-balanced
weights against oversampling with streaming training; a split of the scene
result by signalisation; and an earlier test combining an anchored model's
score with a streaming model's score. Removing ego-vehicle speed from the
tree's input was equivalent within \V{c.margin} points per window (at 4 of the
five budgets) and inconclusive per pedestrian. Adding the ego-future oracle to
the scene was equivalent within \V{c.margin} points, both per window and per
pedestrian. Adding the censored windows to the hazard objective was better per
window (\cref{sec:objresults}) but inconclusive per pedestrian.

\textbf{Inputs.} Scene and behaviour features came from PIE annotations, so
they assume perfect perception of information a camera could see. Noisier,
real-world perception would weaken the \emph{who} result and strengthen the
negative result for \emph{when}. The light feature represents the largest
light in view, which need not control the pedestrian. The finding that lights
add little therefore applies to this feature rather than to right-of-way.
Neither oracle is deployable, and the ego-future oracle is confounded. Its
arms also reached the iteration cap while validation performance was still
improving, so its effect may be understated.

\textbf{Power and scope.} The test split contains \V{peds.cross} crossing
pedestrians. The tree comparisons resolved differences of about \V{c.margin} points.
We used the neural-network recipe despite its failure on a pre-registered
training check: the networks overfit after the first few epochs, and we
report the checkpoint with the best validation AUC. All results come from PIE,
which was recorded in one city in daylight. Our anchored protocol is a
reimplementation, and its scores cannot be compared directly with published
results. We chose the detection metric after obtaining the first window-level
results. Earlier findings motivated the scene and waiting audits, but their
questions were fixed before their own results were obtained.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
The streaming crossing label is the product of two factors, which are the
probability that a pedestrian will cross at all, or \emph{who}, and the
probability that the crossing will begin within a certain horizon, or
\emph{when}. We highlight that the event-anchored protocol measures only
\emph{who}. A model can therefore score well on anchored data while reversing
the ordering of \emph{when}, as we observed on PIE with both a neural network
and boosted trees. We find from our experiments that scene information that a
camera or map could in principle provide raises \emph{who} above a human
intention rating in a post hoc comparison. \emph{When}, by contrast, improves
by at most \V{audit.when.maxgain.abs} and remains low just beyond the horizon.
Furthermore, with the same training windows, a hazard objective does no better
than binary training, consistent with the decomposition. Based on these, we
conclude that evaluations should report \emph{who} and \emph{when} separately,
as further progress in streaming crossing prediction is likely to depend on
information about when pedestrians cross.
